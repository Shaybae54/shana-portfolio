The problem

A recognition problem disguised as a data problem.

The original ask was to add store purchase history to the existing order history page. But the existing page made that impossible. Orders appeared as dense rows of data with product details hidden behind clicks, forcing customers to remember dates or totals just to find what they needed.

Before expanding the feature, we had to fix the foundation. That meant redesigning for how customers actually identify orders: visually, not by recall.

Research
recognition,
not recall

Customers recognize orders. They do not remember them.

I reviewed order history across major retailers and observed customers in a listening lab. The pattern was consistent: people find orders by what they bought, not when they bought it.

Visual

Customers rely on product images to identify orders, not order numbers or dates

3 signals

Product image, order total, and date are the three pieces customers look for immediately

Recognition

Customers recognize an order faster than they can recall the details needed to search for it

Listening lab observation / competitor analysis
where it clicked
The page had all the data. It just was not designed for how people find things.

The reframe: not a feature add. A foundational redesign that made the feature add possible.

What shipped

From spreadsheet rows to visual, scannable cards

Product images lead. Status, date, and total surfaced immediately. The same framework built to support online and in-store purchases without redesigning later.

Reflection
what mattered most

Reframing a feature add into a foundational redesign.

Convincing stakeholders the bigger scope was worth it required a clear argument. The store purchase history launch a year later validated it.

If I had it back, I would have pushed harder for a satisfaction measurement framework at launch so the post-launch story had more specific data.

For the curious
Why recognition beats recall in order history +
Customers identify orders by product images and totals, not order numbers. The listening lab confirmed this across every session. Leading with visual anchors cut search time significantly.
Building the toggle architecture from day one +
The Online / In-Store toggle was designed into the initial framework deliberately. When store purchase history launched a year later, the team extended the experience without rebuilding the system.
How the re-order flow strengthened the business case +
Introducing re-order capability for repeat-purchase categories gave the redesign revenue justification beyond customer satisfaction, which helped earn the roadmap slot.
